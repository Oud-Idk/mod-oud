# What?

Let's define what's happening in your brain. Or more precisely, what's happening in everyone's brain at some point, especially in high school. You might feel it as the weird feeling in your stomach when you want to get close to your crush.

## Difference from Real Love

Real love is knowing how messed up they look when they wake up. Knowing their trauma and history, knowing how they treat waiters and waitresses, and knowing their idiosyncrasies, ahem... unique behaviors, like how they chew with their mouth open, and then deciding to be with them regardless.

A crush is none of that. A crush is noticing a few traits and behaviors that look good from a distance, using the Halo Effect (Dion, Berscheid, & Walster, 1972) to assume everything else about said person is also as amazing, and then falling in love with the character you invented in your own head.

## A Scientific Name?

We call it a crush because we hear it everywhere. It's ubiquitous in any school and social situations. But psychologists have a more precise name for the obsessive, intrusive version of this state _limerence_. It's coined by the psychologist Dr. Dorothy Tennov in her book _Love & Limerence_ (Tennov, 1979).

Don't feel bad about it, as most relationships start with a crush. How do you think your parents found each other?

# How Do You Know If You Have a Case of Being Smitten?

So, we have a name. Limerence. There are multiple indications of this phenomenon. Some of the general stuff includes:

1. Suddenly thinking about your crush no matter the situation.
2. Overanalyzing your body language and your speech in your past interactions with them, so you can derive if there is a chance of reciprocation, while also trying to simulate an imaginary future scenario like a dweeb so you don't fumble.
3. Being happy when they interact with you or being devastated when they don't.

In the modern age, there are specific scenarios to look out for. If you check for more than three of them, you have a crush.

1. You constantly check if they are online on a certain social media.
2. When they leave you on read or don't read your messages at all if they are online, you feel a sting of jealousy and/or sadness.
3. You feel euphoric when they reply to you. That's your brain releasing a ton of dopamine (Fisher, 2004).
   - It activates the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA), which is the same area that activates when you consume cocaine.
4. You see who liked your post every 10 minutes, hoping that there's an off chance that your crush liked it.
5. You scroll on their Instagram page way longer than you would like to admit.
   - This is called _creeping_ (Fox et al., 2013). Creeping is defined as viewing another's page without their knowledge and usually not leaving any markers that would show how in-depth the profile was viewed.
   - Participants in the paper agreed that "Everybody does it, whether they admit it or not."
6. You feel as if your heart skips a beat when there's a notification on your phone, no matter who notified you. Maybe it's your crush?
7. You even stop doomscrolling and start thinking about a future date scenario.
   - We spend about 30% to 50% of our waking life daydreaming (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010). About 71% of those daydreams involve other people (Song & Wang, 2012).
   - If you're anxious about whether they like you back, your daydreams are a subconscious coping mechanism. Poerio et al. (2015) found that we get a happiness boost from these daydreams when we feel disconnected.
   - Your brain can't tell the difference between a real date and the ones you've been imagining for three hours. Well, your conscious mind can, but your lizard brain treats the daydreams as if it's real, making you feel the same feelings (Kosslyn et al., 2001).
8. You catch yourself thinking, "What will my crush think if they see this manifesto?"

W...wait what? Nah nothing, that was just the wind.

# Why in the living hell does Limerence happen

For starters, you have to understand that limerence starts with yourself, not about them. The truth is, limerence is like a dormant virus. It activates when your brain is in the state of readiness.

Especially in high school, your brain is finding someone to be a protagonist—crush—of your life. Perhaps because someone is acting all lovey-dovey, it wants one itself. You might think you were smitten with your crush because they're the one, but they're not. More likely, they're just a fairly decent human being with a few positive traits that your brain latched onto.

To understand this, you have to understand gambling.

## What does gambling have to do with crushes?

That's a great question, and they have a connection. People gamble and lose their life savings. Why? Because of **intermittent reinforcement** (Skinner, 1948). It's basically sometimes getting a reward when a condition is met, like getting only a bit of money when you play a machine.

There's a study done by B.F. Skinner. He placed a pigeon in a cage with a button that dispenses treats. When it always dispenses treats, they only press it enough. When the button does nothing, they realize it quickly, and they move on.

If the button occasionally dispenses a treat, the pigeon keeps pressing it, hoping for the next treat until their beak deteriorates (Skinner, 1948). They think, "What do I do to get the treat coming? Keep pressing? Press it differently? I WANT THE TREAAAAAATTTT!!!"

In this case, you are the pigeon.

## How it all connects

When your crush always replies every time or is always loyal, then the chase is solved. It's nice and warm. But when they take 5 hours to reply, you see them active, they like a post from you, or you think that there's a tiny chance of reciprocation, your brain becomes exactly like that desperate pigeon. You start "pressing the button" differently. You think,

- "Maybe if I wait 10 more minutes."

- "Maybe if I post this one song, they'll see it."

- "Maybe if I even change my profile picture!"

And so on.

Like a slot machine that pays out _sometimes_, when a person only gives signals sometimes, you become obsessed with trying to win their attention.

# How a crush works

Once your brain has found someone that _may_ have a chance of liking you back, even if it's delusional, your brain uses that as a basis and starts inventing stuff to fill in the blanks to get the maximum amount of dopamine every time your crush appears in your brain.

Your brain has the urge to fill in that blank because the behavior was hard-coded when we were simple primates. Your ancestors couldn't just stop thinking about whether that rustle is the wind or a lion without resolving it first. Otherwise, they'd die.

This is known as **Error Management Theory** (Haselton & Buss, 2000). It's better to be wrong and run away from the wind than be wrong and get your gene pool deleted. In the case of a crush, it's flipped. Instead of grave bodily injury, it's dopamine and getting your genes passed.

# Stages of a Crush

"So, how does this play out?" you wonder. Based on "extensive" research, which involved me staring out of the window for exactly 3 hours 14 minutes, and reading Dr Tennov's work, we can probably break this down.

## 1. The Glint

It starts when a specific person does something unique and special to you. Maybe they waved at you, smiled at you, or you just randomly noticed how their hair looks under the sunlight. These signs are used by your brain to convince itself that there is a chance that they like you. And suddenly, this person, who was a normie moments ago, becomes your point of interest.

Dr. Tennov quoted, "Limerence enters your life pleasantly... Someone takes on a special meaning... some look or word or gesture that is interpreted to indicate possible responsiveness, seems necessary".

## 2. The Halo Effect

Now, this is the part where your brain just makes up stuff. You clearly know their flaws, but you just think of them as positives. For example,

- Their messy handwriting isn't bad; it's "uniquely them."

- They send a text full of mistakes. They aren't sloppy, they are "energetic to talk to you."

- Their blurry photo in their Instagram Story isn't bad; it's "authentic."

- They take six hours to reply with only one word. They aren't uninterested; they are "mysterious."

I could give plenty more examples, but it's better if we just move on. Besides, you get the point. Your brain does this because a perfect person gives a bigger dopamine hit than a normal person, let alone a disinterested one.

Tennov uses Stendhal's "salt mine branch" analogy: you take a plain twig (the person), throw it into the salt mine of your mind, and it comes out shimmering with crystals that look like diamonds. Or, in this case, your brain takes a normie and covers them in special and positive characteristics until that person looks likeable.

## 3. The Increase in Intensity

This is the bird-brain stage. CAW-CAW, let's press this button that I know will only sometimes work and will get me addicted, but I don't care.

A crush needs a specific amount of difficulty to grow. Tennov notes that if they are too easy, i.e., if they say "I love you" on the first date, it doesn't grow; it usually fades into secure love once you both have committed. If they are playing too hard to get, you give up. If they are hard to get but not _too_ difficult to give up on, like that (not so) small space when you think "I think they like me" and "I'm not sure," that's the sweet spot where feelings grow.

Tennov quoted, "Your degree of involvement increases if obstacles are externally imposed or if you doubt [your crush]'s feelings for you... With some degree of doubt its intensity rises further" (Tennov, 1979).

Maybe initially, your preoccupation starts at 5%. Then, it goes up to 50%. When you see a gift-worthy item, you might think, "I should get this. Totally not for my crush, hehe!" This is the most likely point when people think that someone is having a crush. Then 80%.

## 4. The Point of No Return

And finally, 100%. Or close to it. All roads lead to Rome, they say.

This is when the thoughts become intrusive. When limerence takes over your brain, you cannot stop thinking about them, whether it's ~~writing this~~ doing your dishes, playing games, or doom-scrolling (that's where the future date scenario came from). You start replaying a specific moment of time with your crush (i.e., a playful elbow nudge) and scrutinize it to look out for sub-text or a hidden confession of some sort.

### 4A. Scripting

You suddenly find yourself looking in a mirror, practicing delivering jokes with a charming smile (when in reality, it's crap). Maybe also practicing laughing (personally, I can't imagine how one would do that) or giving pickup lines.

"Oh, dear, are you a Wi-Fi signal? Because I'm really feeling a special connection," I said. It was indeed cringy. I should probably come up with a different one, perhaps with my own nerd flair to signal them who I am. I tried again with a wall lean. "Is your name Bluetooth? Because I think we've paired successfully." Why am I practicing when I will probably never say them to my crush out loud? I don't even know myself.

I need to be stopped.

## 5. The Body Speaks!

This is where it stops being fun and starts being stressful and a bit sad. Dr. Tennov interviewed some limerent people. They said that their chest literally hurts when they know their limerent object (LO, or your crush) might not feel the same way. This is where we get the term "heartbroken" from.

Your hands might shake if they enter your room. Hell, you can even stutter or freeze. You are afraid of looking like a buffoon in front of your crush, so you get nervous, and you promptly forget your native language.

You might also be so obsessed with your crush that you may forget to do your homework as your body is stuck in the state of fight or flight, thinking whether they are a lion or a god.

If you are stuck in this stage, your immune cells, specifically your T-cells and your B-cells, might weaken because of the stress (Alotiby, 2024).

## 6. The Finale

This is the turning point. You finally notice their flaws or realize they're a jerk after God knows how long and decided that they are not worth falling for. Or you go to a date with them and decide that they are worth for what they are. The mystery disappears, and you finally try the harder (but much better) work of [real love](#difference-from-real-love).

# Dopamine

Most people think that this is the pleasure chemical. They think it's the one that makes the brain go, "Mm... this is great. I'm happy!"

They're wrong.

Dopamine is the anticipatory chemical. It's the "Do it again" chemical. It's for cravings and the love of learning, not for satisfaction. That's the job for serotonin and other endogenous opioids. (Berridge & Robinson, 1998)

## The Anticipation

As I stated just now, you will get anxious when a notification pops up on your phone if you have a crush. That's the dopamine in action. It doesn't matter who notified you. You only care that there's a slight chance that your crush is the one that sent it.

> "Oh my! I really, really hope it is my crush replying to me! Oh, dear."

If it's actually them, your brain connects that dopamine spike to the notification sound. If it's just your mom, then the dopamine drops, and you feel a bit down. Consequently, you go back to doomscrolling on your bed forever.

## Again!

Dopamine is evolution's creation to make sure we don't sit in a cave doing absolutely nothing. We'd die as we don't find food. It rewards repeatedly seeking.

- Find edible plants? DO IT AGAIN!

- Find a watering hole? DO IT AGAIN!

- Find a person who smiled at you once in a hallway? THAT'S THE ONE. DO IT AGAIN! AGAIIIINNNN!!!

## The Prediction Error

There is this thing called the Reward Prediction Error. Here's the gist of it.

- If you expect a text and get one, your dopamine is _meh_.

- If you don't expect a text and get one, your dopamine goes SKY HIGH!

- Finally, if you expect a text but don't get one, _I feel sorry for you._

### The Math Behind it

Think of it as the numerical difference between reality and your expectations. This is represented by $\delta = R - V$ where $\delta$ is the prediction error, $R$ is the reward, and $V$ is the expected value.

- 10 (receiving a text) - 10 (expecting a text) = 0 (meh)  
  this is why being stable can be boring.

- 10 (receiving a text) - 0 (not expecting anything) = 10 (YAY!)

- 0 (not receiving a text) - 10 (expecting a text) = -10 (NO!)

- 10 (receiving a compliment through text) - (-10) (expecting a mocking remark) = 20 (Give me a moment as I process this one)

Your brain basically releases dopamine based on the _difference_ in expectation. It's basically our brain's way to make us pay attention to anomalies. (Schultz, Dayan, & Montague, 1997)

## The Love of Learning

Dopamine is also related to novelty and learning. It's what drives (some of) us to continue learning Mathematics or Code. In this case, learning about your crush.

When you have a crush, they are like a puzzle.

- What does that emoji mean?

- Why are they wearing this shirt today?

- Do they like this specific snack that I like?

Every time you solve a problem, your brain gives you a bit of a treat, aka dopamine. This is why you can spend four hours looking at their Instagram page from 5 years ago. Your brain thinks it's doing something important.

Your brain seeks out new information because new usually means probably useful for survival in the Stone Age, and even now. When you finally solve how crushes work, or a new detail about your crush, your brain treats it as a W. (Bromberg-Martin, Matsumoto, & Hikosaka, 2010)

# Why We Hate Rejection

## A. The Evolutionary View

Back in the days when all of us were simple primates, being liked wasn't purely ego; it was for survival too.

If you get accepted into a tribe, you get food and protection. If you are lonely, get rejected, or kicked out of a tribe, you become a tiger's snack and your genes won't get passed.

When you get rejected by your crush, your brain isn't thinking, "Oh, they're probably busy." It thinks, "THE TRIBE HAS ABANDONED ME! I WILL GET EATEN BY A TIGER! I AM GOING TO STARVE! NOOOOO!!!"

This also explains why you have to look cool and impressive in front of your crush. Your brain thinks that your crush is like a tribe that gives protection.

## B. The Biological View

If you get rejected, it isn't just sadness; it is a withdrawal, like quitting smoking cold turkey (suddenly).

### Addiction

You see, when you are limerent, your brain constantly receives dopamine. You are basically a drug addict, where the drug is your crush. When they reject you, the supply is cut off.

Your brain goes haywire. Instead of giving up, your brain goes into a state of frustration attraction, also known as adversity-heightened passion, as it is called in the paper. In a 2010 study, Fisher et al. found that looking at a rejecter continues to activate the reward center (VTA). The paper says when a reward is delayed, the dopamine neurons don't suddenly stop; they actually continue (Fisher et al., 2010).

## What part of the brain actually activates?

There was a belief that social pain triggers the same area of the brain as physical pain (Kross et al., 2011). That's where the saying "a broken heart is the same as a broken leg" comes from. But the field is shying away from that conclusion.

There was an initial study where the correlation between dACC activation and social distress was as high as $r=.88$ (Eisenberger et al., 2003). Then, there were debates whether that finding is all BS or factual (Vul et al., 2009; Lieberman et al., 2009).

Vul basically argued that no one should be surprised when I take the shortest people from classroom A, the tallest people from classroom C, and the in-between at classroom B, and saying that the letters of the classroom correlate with height. That's a bit of an overexaggeration, but you get the point. Ultimately, what came out was yes, that data was inflated, but the biology still stands.

Recent meta-analyses show that rejection instead consistently activates the Default Mode Network, areas used for self-reflection and trying to understand others' thoughts, rather than the simple physical pain centers, the dACC (Vijayakumar et al., 2017). Therefore, instead of hurting, one would think, "What did I do wrong? _\*Trying to remember what happened this year for any mistakes\*_ Oh no, they hate me now!?" It's less like a broken leg and more of a puzzle.

# Jealousy

So, you think that jealousy is a social construct. The truth is it comes deeply from biology.

You see, jealousy is usually defined as a _state_ caused by a perceived threat to a relationship and motivates behavior aimed at preventing and/or fighting the threat (Daly et al., 1982).

As documented by Daly, males in species with internal fertilization experience paternity uncertainty because they aren't the ones carrying the baby.

Different animals deal with this differently. For example, young cubs by other males are
_systematically_ killed, as in lions (Bertram, 2009). In the case of humans, evolution gave us jealousy.

It does that to prevent us from raising the wrong kids. If males did raise the wrong kids, which aren't their own, they are raising kids that don't have their genes, and as we know, the meaning of life in biology is passing down genes, and they'd fail in their only task in life. As a result, evolutionary theory predicted that sexual jealousy would be especially present in males.

## How it differs from Envy

Envy usually occurs when a person doesn't have another's superior quality, achievement, or possession and either desires it or wishes the other person lacked it, while jealousy, by contrast, occurs when a person fears losing an important relationship with another person to a rival (Parrott, 1993).

According to a study done by Parrott, envy is usually associated with the disapproval of the feeling itself and longing (of what the other person has), while jealousy is associated with betrayal, fear of loss, and uncertainty.

It is important to remember that they are not mutually exclusive, as one can absolutely feel angry about the unfairness of life whether they are jealous or envious.

## Why in the World Do We Get Jealous Even Though It Isn't Official?

Well, in your mind, it is. Tennov lists "acute longing for reciprocation" as a building block of limerence. The smitten person is _always_ observing, constantly analyzing their crush's actions for signs of any reciprocation. A rival can threaten this hope, so they feel jealous.

What I described in jealousy section for human males is sexual jealousy, and it differs from limerent jealousy, which focuses on whether the person returns the feelings. Do not confuse these two, though limerent jealousy usually precedes sexual jealousy, and they can happen at the same time.

Tennov notes that jealousy is **NOT** a measure of limerence. You can be limerent without jealousy (a crush with no rival) or jealous without limerence.

## Why Do We Want Things Other People Want?

Research has shown that we want things that other people want. This is called mimetic desire (Garrels, 2005).

First, you have to know that imitation is basically like an instinct. We can see this as babies can imitate facial expressions mere minutes after being born.

Plus, we have mirror neurons, which are neurons that fire when we do something and see someone else do that thing. Our brains are programmed from the start to react to other people's actions that matches our own. This is essential to empathy and language, but it also makes people want to imitate others too.

Garrels points out that imitation is more goal-oriented. Babies don't simply copy movements. They copy the meaning and intentions behind it. Turns out, when two people imitate each other's desire for something, they become rivals. This is _Acquisitive Mimesis_ (Garrels, 2005). And this is how you get jealousy.

# Attachment Theory

Basically, it's a theory that explains how humans attach to each other, and it's particularly influenced by early experiences (Cassidy, 2013).

Human babies are born totally helpless. Because of this, we developed attachment. Its only job is to make sure the baby stays close enough to the caregiver so they don't die, duh. In Cassidy's paper, they name it the "Attachment Behavioral System," which sounds a lot more complicated than it needs to be.

## The Parent's Job in This Scenario

In this theory, parents have two jobs (Cassidy, 2013):

- Being a secure base. If you know the base is safe and has supplies, you feel brave enough to explore the world and that you can always return to your parents.

- Being a safe haven. If something scary shows up, like hurting, being scared or stressed, you go back to your parents to hide and recharge.

Kind of sweet, isn't it?

## The Building Stage

The paper says by the time you are 1 year old (yes, that early), you already have a method (Internal Working Model) in your head on attachment based on how your parents treated you.

- If your parents treated you well, you are secure. "I'm screwed, but someone will help me. I am worth helping."

- If your parents treated you like a garbage can, you will be insecure. "I'm in a peculiar situation. I'm on my own. People are mean!"

How is this related to crushes? Because those methods stick. Really stubbornly. We have the core system built in (like instincts) but we need to build the system for attachment because we don't have one. We do this through early relationships with our caretaker, like our parents. Our brain adapts easily especially during the first three years of our life (Marzola, 2023).

## The Panic Slider

We have the HPA axis, which is like a slider for panic.

- If your parents calm you down, the HPA axis reduces, which teaches babies how to turn it off independently.

- Inversely, if your parents don't calm you down or neglects you, the HPA axis is stuck in the max setting, and they don't know how to turn it off yet (Gunnar & Quevedo, 2007).
  - If the baby doesn't learn how to turn it off, then they will have a hard time turning it off later in life.

  - They can neglect you by being workaholics, emotionally unavailable, or even being anxious themselves!

## Good News! You're Not Totally Screwed

This doesn't mean it's impossible to reverse bad parenting (Wallin, 2007). One can, for example, take therapy to reduce it or find healthy relationships to finally learn how to move that slider down.

# Why "Maybe" is Worse Than "No"

So, you finally did it. You confessed. I guess. Congratulations? But they gave a non-committal answer.

> Oh, that's so sweet! But later? I'm actually really busy right now.

Then, you went home and cannot stop thinking about the confession.

> What the hell does that "maybe" mean? Does she like me? Does she think that I'm only an ordinary friend? AAAH!

Or you confessed online, and they left you on read.

You think about it so much it impairs your sleep. And you finally wonder, "Why am I getting all of these thoughts?"

Well, congratulations, you have just been handed an unfinished task. It's not just you; it's actually a phenomenon in humans, and we call it the Zeigarnik Effect (Zeigarnik, 1938).

## What's the Zeigarnik Effect?

Back in the 1920s (yes, that long ago), a psychologist named Bluma Zeigarnik noticed that waiters could memorize any unpaid orders perfectly _up until_ the bill was paid. Like everything was forgotten. Zeigarnik found this fascinating, so she designed experiments to know more about it. Then, she wrote a paper and published it as _Über das Behalten von erledigten und unerledigten Handlungen_, or what English speakers call it, _On Finished and Unfinished Tasks_.

It is a habit where people remember unfinished tasks a lot better than finished tasks. She tested this hypothesis by doing some experiments that show that uncompleted mental tasks (such as puzzles or constructing cardboard boxes) induce a tendency to experience intrusive thoughts and are 90% more likely to be recollected than finished tasks.

## Affective Rumination vs. Problem-Solving Pondering

Cropley & Zijlstra (2011) made it pretty clear that Affective Rumination is different from Problem-Solving Pondering.

1. Affective Rumination is basically like a state where negative, intrusive, and repeating thoughts about an unfinished task appear. "Oh my God. Did I fumble hard? Why haven't they replied? Did I look too desperate? Help!" This is unproductive and doesn't go anywhere. This is seen as the bridge that ruins sleep for employees (Syrek et al., 2017)

2. Problem-Solving Pondering is a form of long thinking about previous work, and how it can be improved, but does
   _not_ involve negative thoughts. "In 3 days, if they haven't gotten back to me, I'll send them a bump. If they don't reply anyway, I'll just continue coding. Shucks!"

## Why Vague Answers are Bad

When people leave work with unfinished tasks, they are more than twice as likely to spiral into Affective Rumination $\beta_w=.18,~SE=.05$ than to trigger the calm Problem-Solving Pondering $\beta_w=.07,~SE=.04$ (Syrek, 2017). Consequently, if someone confesses to you, and you give a vague answer, that person literally cannot sleep because they might be ruminating about it.

## So, What?

It's pretty easy. You should give a direct Yes/No answer. You can think for a while, but not defer it until the following days. And by definition, if you write your thoughts on a piece of paper, it will kill this effect.

# The Triangular Theory of Love

No, it's not a love triangle. This is different. Sternberg's (1986) triangular theory of love is a framework that explores how human affection actually works. The vertices of the triangle are defined as:

- **Intimacy**: This is the emotional vertex. The closeness, the warmth, and sharing secrets. It's the bestie part.
- **Passion**: This is the motivation vertex. It's the butterflies and the physical attraction.
- **Commitment**: This is the cognitive vertex. It's when you decide consciously if you want to commit to it. It means during the short term, you choose to love that person, and during the long term, you choose to stick around.

Using that, we can define 8 types of love.

- **Non-love** (-): Strangers.
- **Liking** (Intimacy): Your friends.
- **Infatuated Love** (Passion): Everything we just discussed.
- **Empty Love** (Commitment): Marriages where everyone does nothing.
- **Romantic Love** (Intimacy + Passion): High school rom-com moment.
- **Companionate Love** (Intimacy + Commitment): Deep, long-term friendship. Very wholesome. Also found in a happy, long-term marriage.
- **Fatuous Love** (Passion + Commitment): Instant romance. You meet, get obsessed, exclusively date after one week, get married in one month, and then realize you are an idiot. Fatuous meaning foolish.
- **Consummate Love** (Passion + Intimacy + Commitment): Only in legends, alongside true test-driven developers, code working first-try, and high-schoolers who don't overthink.

These components are continuous, not discrete. What it means is that you can have a bit of something but also a lot of something at the same time. Think of it as like the shape of a triangle. If it's equilateral, it's balanced. Otherwise, if it's a scalene, something is off and unbalanced. And the area of the triangle is the total amount.

Satisfaction is linked through your expectation and reality. If your **Real Triangle** matches your **Ideal Triangle**, you will be more satisfied. And if you feel you are 100% committed, but your partner feels otherwise, it will fail.

# How to Know if That Person has a Crush On Me?

You want to look for body language. Specifically, subconscious signs of proximity and mirroring.

1. Proximity. If you are in a room of 50 people and there's always one that just seems to be around you once, it's a coincidence, twice is a fluke, but thrice and more is a pattern.

2. Mirroring. Remember the [mirror neurons](#why-do-we-want-things-other-people-want)? Well, they will
   **subconsciously do what you do**. At that section, I wrote that the role of the mirror neurons are to enhance social connection and empathy. Usually, at least. When you lean back, they lean back. When you drink water, they drink too. They are unconsciously trying to tell you, "I feel safe with you. I want to be like you." In hindsight, that's super sweet!
   - A study placed participants on an online date (Li et al., 2024). When the researchers artificially increased their partner's smile, the participant thought, "Hey, this person likes me a lot!"
   - When both are smiling at the same time, romantic attraction was the highest.
   - Interestingly, when both people had decreased smiles, it was still rated higher than when one is smiling and the other is not. Wrong energy kills the vibe and makes things awkward!

3. You might think that the eyebrows are useless for romantic attraction, but a study found that our eyebrows are specifically designed for social connection (Godinho et al., 2018). For instance, when someone **raises their eyebrow briefly** when they notice you, they are likely surprised and pleased that you were there.

4. If their pupils are enlarged when seeing you, they are definitely interested.
   - No, it's not just Disney logic. Kret (2018) did a study where people were asked to draw pupils on a happy face and an angry face. The happy face has larger pupils than the angry face.
   - They dilate from mental effort (Kahneman & Beatty, 1966). You see, the pupils dilate when you are in a high stress situation, as you need a lot of visual information, like letting more light in (Mathôt, 2018).
   - And also because they want to appear innocent and baby-like.
   - Don't take this as the _only_ evidence. Make sure you are not in a dark room, or they aren't on meds.

5. A smile and other facial expressions can be faked as we have massive control over them, but **the legs are rather hard to fake**. Ekman & Friesen (1969) showed that because humans barely pay attention to what our feet are doing and assume that nobody is looking at them, our feet won't lie.
   - Mehrabian (1968) found that body and leg orientation (and how much you lean forward) is a great indicator of said person's interest, whereas angling your legs away is a great indicator of intense dislike.
   - Vacharkulksemsuk et al. (2016) also said that open postures (i.e., open leg and foot placement) significantly boost romantic attraction during initial encounters.
   - Ironically, since you have read this section, you will consciously monitor your limbs, and when observers start watching feet instead of faces, the whole thing repeats.

# Blushing

## What is Blushing?

As you all know, it is the reddening of the face, ears, neck, and even the upper chest caused by the dilation of blood vessels near your skin (Leary et al., 1992). The blush region is only on specific areas of the skin, not everywhere (Frijda, 1986).

## Why?

Blushing happens when we receive attention that we simply don't want, like getting more praise than you are comfortable with, as you appear to get more attention than you deserved. It's not simply becoming more self-aware (Kaufman, 1989; Tomkins, 1963). On top of that, if you simply accept the attention, it will make you arrogant, making you blush to appear less smug. And people also will look away from people who are blushing as they find it uncomfortable seeing other people suffer, which, in turn, reduces attention.

It also acts as a non-verbal apology. It shows people that you messed up, hoping that they will forgive you (see Cupach, Metts, & Hazelton, 1986; Schlenker, 1980). Furthermore, it is sincerer than any spoken apology as you cannot control a blush.

## Why Must It Be The Face?

The paper says that the blood vessels in the face are more relaxed and can hold a much larger volume of blood than other parts. Plus, it has specific receptors that can trigger vasodilation, which is pretty unusual. And it just so happens to be in a more visible area.

## How Is It Related to Crushing?

In the context of crushes, your brain is, for some reason, trying to apologize for existing near someone so high-value and... cute (look back at the smug part) while trying to get their attention. It's almost a paradox.

# Conflict Management

You got a special person. But you are in a fight with them. Maybe because they like pineapple on pizza (it's a disgrace, I know), or perhaps you think they don't put in effort. How do you deal with it?

Since it is inevitable, we will focus on _how_ to actually handle a conflict like a champ.

## How Conflicts Lead To a Very Bad Day

Gottman (1994) noted that only four characteristics (The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse) can predict whether a couple will divorce with up to 90% accuracy. Those are:

- **Criticism**: Attacking the partner's character or personality rather than a specific behavior. It's different from a complaint for that reason.
  - "You never do the dishes, you're so lazy!"
  - It's an attempt to be righteous for their ego, even though objectively, they might be wrong.
- **Contempt**: Negative thoughts about their partner that persist. The most corrosive horseman. Usually acting from superiority.
  - "You're such an asshole."
  - It's to make the partner feel worthless. The single greatest indicator of a relationship failure and even physical illness.
- **Defensiveness**: Denying any wrongdoing.
  - "It's not my fault the house is dirty—I did <u>my</u> chores!"
  - It's an attempt to dodge responsibility. Maybe you are too lazy or too superior to be responsible or wrong. And also ego.
- **Stonewalling**: The listener just stops listening.
  - **DPA (Diffuse Physiological Arousal)** causes this. When our heart rate is above 100 bpm during a seated conversation, we cannot process any more information. Our IQ drops and ooga-booga brain takes over.
  - Nods. Looks away. Or acts like a stone wall.
  - It's usually a survival mechanism, but it usually conveys a lack of actually engaging. It makes the other partner feel ignored, because they probably are, shutting down any chance of resolving the actual issue.

## The Fix

The antidotes in order, by Gottman again, are:

- **Gentle Startups & Specific Complaints**: Instead of being direct, soften the approach. "I feel" statements can be an excellent method of starting a conversation about a need or want.
  - "I feel frustrated because the dishes aren’t done. Can we figure something out together?"
- **Validation & Fondness**: Showing gratitude towards partners. It is effective in growing appreciation, no matter if we're in a conflict or not. Recognize each other's strengths.
  - "I understand talking about money can be really stressful for you. Thanks for having the discussion with me."
  - It's not all about agreeing; it just means that your partner's feelings are valid.
  - Validation is like giving them a snack so they won't go
    _grr_. It makes the partner feel safe and stop attacking.
- **Nondefensive Listening & Taking Responsibility**: Instead of defending directly, you could listen until the end and take responsibility. A fight is rarely caused by just one person. Both people will play a part in the conflict, and being able to identify what you took part in can prevent defensiveness.
  - "I shouldn't have raised my voice when you disagreed with me. I'm sorry."
- **Physiological Soothing**: Instead of fighting when your mind is cluttered, the best way to deal with this is to take a break for at least 20 minutes, but no more than 24 hours.
  - "I feel overwhelmed right now. Can we pause and come back later?"

# Sudden Repulsion Syndrome

## What is that?

Also known as "the ick", it is defined as a sudden and strong disgust toward a romantic partner or crush, usually because of behaviors that might come across as low mate quality (Collisson et al., 2025). It is shown a lot in pop culture. For example, in the show _Love Island_, participants show a repulsion to people who have an overly bright smile or to awkward hand holding.

It is also influenced by people around you. For example, observing others' choices can lead people to internalize those ideas due to cultural norms and the like.

## Why do we have it?

It may be because humans are more sensitive to shitty partners than good ones (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). We are thought to watch for traits that we might think of as an incompatibility or a relational risk. And even then, small, slightly inconveniencing habits may also be a protective mechanism by our mind to reject 'unfit' partners before investing their time completely.

Evolutionarily, we are wired to do this as a false-positive (i.e. accepting a bad partner) can lead to tiredness, and carry long-term problems, whereas a false negative (i.e. rejecting a good partner) is simply just a missed opportunity (Haselton, M. G. & Galerin, A., 2013).

# The Five Love Languages

People usually define them as quality time, words of affirmation, gifts, acts of service, and physical touch. The truth is it's mostly bullshit, and there is no empirical evidence that they work. Recent research shows that love languages do not predict relationship satisfaction (Impett et al., 2024). Instead, a better way to look at love languages is their Perceived Partner Responsiveness, in other words, whatever your partner wants and feels!

## Why Did We Make It?

Humans like to put things into boxes (Fiske & Taylor, 1984; Macrae & Bodenhausen, 2000). You see, the world is complex, and your brain is lazy, so it takes shortcuts wherever it can, so we naturally force people into discrete boxes, but people are way more nuanced than that.

Listen here, I am a man of science, but this isn't. _The Five Love Languages: How to Express Heartfelt Commitment to Your Mate_ (Chapman, 1992) was invented in 1992 by a pastor with zero empirical research and no psychology certificates (I don't have one either, but I actually look up empirical research), yet people still think this is The (Shitty) Bible for loving in Pop Psych.

## Debunking

Impett, Park, & Muise (2024) debunked these assumptions like so:

- People might think that each person has a _primary_ love language  
  Reality: people just want to love in any way they can. Even if they are forced to choose one love language, it's meaningless!
- There are _five_ love languages  
  Reality: those five love languages are close to arbitrary. There are lots of ways humans express love.
- 'Speaking' the _same_ love language leads to greater relationship quality  
  Reality: No consistent correlations exist. Showing affection in _any_ way improves relationship quality anyway.

## So What Should We Do Instead?

Think of love as a **balanced diet** (Impett, Park, & Muise, 2024). I know, it may be silly, but it's the easiest way to understand it without combusting.

- You need **all** of them. You can't rely solely on carbs (quality time), your (emotional) body needs protein (words of affirmation), healthy fats (acts of service), and vitamins (physical touch).
- A marathon runner needs more carbs before a big race. Likewise, if your partner is going through something tough, they might need extra 'supplements' of _physical affection_ to get through their struggle.
- If your partner is feeling insecure, they might need _supplemental iron_ (extra appreciation and validation) to feel safe in a relationship.

Remember, there are no miracle fat loss supplements[^1] or cheat codes for love. Real romance is about responsiveness. Paying attention to what gesture your partner needs and giving it to them.

# Long Distance Relationships

Holy shit lmfao, the papers actually explain a lot. If you only have the attention span to read this paragraph, please listen to me and tread carefully in your LDR.

## What is an LDR?

Ln LDR is not technically defined as two people separated by a 12-hour timezone difference, but rather, it is defined by their reality (and convenience).

A **long-distance relationship** is any relationship where it is _"difficult or impossible for partners to see each other in person every day due to geographical distance"_ (Dargie et al., 2013; Holtzman et al., 2021; Maguire, 2007).

The key here is _impossible for partners to see each other in person every day_. If your partner lives 80 km away, but you have quick transportation that makes it practical to see each other every day, that's not an LDR, while even if your partner only lives half the distance, but the logistics make it impossible, it is an LDR.

### What's the Difference Between an LDR and a Geographically Close Relationship (GCR)?

In a GCR, if you live half an hour away from your partner and can't be bothered to hang out together, that's your choice (Stafford & Merolla, 2007). In an LDR, by contrast, whether it's a city away or in a different continent, you just cannot hang out together to have a spontaneous dinner (Dellmann-Jenkins et al., 1994).

### Not All LDRs Are Created Equal

According to papers that I read, LDRs are usually split into three different categories (Dargie et al., 2013; Maguire, 2007):

- **Transitory LDRs** are ones where there is a fixed end date (e.g. "We graduate in May").
- **Perpetual LDRs** are LDRs where there will be no date for when or if you will ever live together (Maguire, 2007). _This is usually where stress peaks_ because as I said 100 times in this paper/blog post/whatever, humans hate uncertainty.
- **Cyclic LDRs** are couples who cyclically shift from being close together to being long distance (e.g., college students who live together during school year but go home for summer break) (Dargie et al., 2013)

## Halo Effect times 100

Yeah, so, turns out, if you can't see people that often, you invent EVEN MORE stuff than with a conventional crush.

Remember the Halo Effect? That's where your brain sees one good thing and assumes they must be perfect and/or reinterpreting their flaws as a good thing (Tennov, 1979). In LDR, it's exponentially increased. Researchers call this
**romantic idealization**. It is when you describe your partner and relationship in an absurdly positive way (Fowers et al., 1996; Stafford & Merolla, 2007).

It's so extreme that LDR couples consistently score _significantly higher_ on love, idealistic distortion and perceived agreement than ones who live in the same city (Stafford & Merolla, 2007).

"Why?" You might ask.

---

### No Mundanes to See

In a local relationship, you realize that your partner is... not exactly a deity. You see them chew loudly or get irrationally angry otherwise. In an LDR, however, you cannot see their full 'behavioral repertoire' (Miller et al., 2003; Stafford & Merolla, 2007). Because you don't see their mundane flaws, your brain invents the blind spots with good shits.

### Selective Self-Presentation & Conflict Avoidance

In other words, they filter themselves so they're not annoying, angry, or end up starting an argument (Sahlstein, 2004; Stafford & Merolla, 2007). People in such relationships are usually on their best behavior (i.e. charming, loving, avoiding taboo topics) and not showing their _true, true_ selves, therefore you end up dating your imagination.

### Texts are Ambiguous

When communications happens only in text messages and/or video calls, humans end up doing **behavioral adaptation** (Jiang & Hancock, 2013). You share deeper shits to compensate for distance and the lack of meeting up, and your partner ends up misinterpreting those messages as a sign of soulmates (Jiang & Hancock, 2013; Walther, 1996). For example, a simple "thinking of you \:3" might end up being overthought by the other party as a poem.

---

Due to a combination of aforementioned factors, LDR couples actually end up reporting **equal or higher** relationship intimacy than couples who live together (Jiang & Hancock, 2013; Stafford & Merolla, 2007).

## You Meet Up

And that's exactly when your imagination shatters like a poorly coded JavaScript applet with no regard for type safety and, for that matter, any rational logic. If your attachment is not grounded

In a longitudinal study[^2] of long-distance couples who finally end up living near each other, researchers found that **couples who reunited were TWICE as likely to break up** compared to couples who stayed long-distance (Stafford & Merolla, 2007).

### Your Imagination Shatters

Seeing people's behavior in real life nukes your [Halo Effect Times 100](#halo-effect-times-100). It deletes your fantasy like a programmer looking at past code and declares that it should be deleted into the abyss.

When researchers asked couples who used to be in an LDR about their experience, **40% reported discovering new, previously unknown facts** about their partner after moving close together from an LDR (Stafford et al., 2006; Stafford & Merolla, 2007). Oh, good news, it's positive right? Nope, it's about **four times more likely to be negative than positive**.

You might find out that they might be a stranger who bickers about who is going to do the dishes, doesn't look as good-looking as they appear from an Instagram filter, and has actual annoying habits that they hid during any voice calls.

### The Selective Best Behavior Can't Stay

Sure, during the LDR phase, you are on your best behavior every phone call, but in real life, you simply can't keep that act for long, like actually having to resolve conflicts normally instead of hanging up, or doing chores together instead of slacking off.

IN FACT, LDRs ARE ACTUALLY VERY
**STABLE**... as long as you don't meet up together (Stafford & Merolla, 2007).

[^2] A longitudinal study is a research method where scientists test the exact same people or things over time.

## Perpetual LDRs

[Distance doesn't kill LDRs. Open-ended uncertainty does](#why-maybe-is-worse-than-no). In fact, it might be harder as a straight 'no' isn't exactly possible in this circumstance (i.e., "I know them well, and I really, really want to meet up with them!").

Maguire (2007) found that couples who feel uncertain about when (or even if) they are going to live in the same city are going to be ridiculously stressed out. Humans can survive a 12-hour time difference if there's certainty that they'll be meeting up in a reasonable timeframe, but will be stressed out if there's no fixed date for getting together.

TL;DR: have a fixed date for meeting up, please :>

## Advice

So, you want to stay in an LDR? I gotchu. Researchers (and me) don't want you feeling perpetually lonely in a landscape where people are fake, so you can do the following:

1. **Set an end date to for when you will transition into a conventional relationship.** Agree on a realistic timeline for when, where, and how you will live together (Maguire, 2007). Without a clear plan, there's ambiguity, and with ambiguity, you'll go insane.

2. **Text small, mundane updates instead of forcing 3-hour voice calls.**[^3] You don't need a 2-hour video call every single night if you both stare into each other's souls through your magic rectangle when you don't have things to say. Instead, try texting frequently ("good morning stupid", "meow"). It fosters relationship satisfaction by creating a 'virtual co-presence' (i.e. existing together). That way, talking won't feel like a chore anymore (Holtzman et al., 2021).

3. **Purposely talk about boring/flawed things.** Force yourself to [be grounded in reality](#no-mundanes-to-see) (Stafford & Merolla, 2007). Share your frustrations, your pet peeves, and your annoyances. If you only interact positively, you aren't dating a real human; rather, you're dating your imagination.

4. **Prepare for the awkwardness when you finally meet up.** It _will_ be awkward or surreal, so no trying to avoid that. Your brain is adjusting to the physical, touchable human in front of you and not just s packets[^4] (Stafford et al., 2006). It's like a jet lag of some sort, and it's normal.

[^3] If you genuinely want to talk, then go on!  
[^4] I'm not a fan of TCP packets. If you're going to stream affection over the internet, at least use QUIC.

# Misattribution of Arousal (Why Theme Parks Make Great Dates)

> Physiological Arousal + Cognitive Label = Emotion (Schachter & Singer, 1962)

**You actually like your crush more if they're involved when doing something scary.**

Dutton & Aron (1974) ran a study on guys where they had to cross a wobbly bridge and a sturdy bridge. An attractive female researcher approached each participant and asked him to fill out a survey, which included making a short, dramatic story from a picture of a woman covering her face with one hand. After the survey was finished, she would say, "I'm in a rush, but if you want to hear more about the results later, here's my number." It's done on the two bridges.

The scary bridge guys wrote much more sensual stories than the guys on the sturdy bridge. And also, the scary bridge guys called way more often. The same experiment was conducted with a male researcher. This time, almost nobody called him.

Schachter & Singer (1962) argued that real emotions should have an arousal component and a label as to why it happens. This is precisely why the bridge experiment worked.

If someone is aroused, but there is no immediate explanation, they will use the environment to label it. If there is an obvious explanation, they will not label their feelings using the environmental cues. Therefore, we can conclude that the guys on the bridge misattributed their racing hearts to intense romantic attraction.

# Some Fun Facts, FAQs & Tips

1. **The Sweaty T-Shirt Study (Wedekind et al., 1995)**: Did you know that you are attracted to the scents of people with different immune systems with you? It's to make formidable off-spring.
   - If the woman is on hormonal birth control, this effect often disappears!
   - _Do not use this as a pickup line._ Do not just go to a person, SNIFF obnoxiously, and say, "Mmm, your MHC suggests we'd have a good offspring." You will either be a dork or a creep.
2. **Holding Hand and Physical Contact (Coan et al., 2006)**: Did you know that holding your partner's hand calmed your hypothalamus? That also means your immune system will be stronger!
3. **The Situationship Rule**: So, they decided to declare you "strictly as a friend", but their behaviors are all couple-y. That, my friend, is a situationship. Just take that as a no and retract. If they decided to retract what they said, they _will_ come.
4. **The Emotional Trade**: Always trade for the hot highs for a warm security. It's much better that way.
5. **Who Wrote This**: I just thought of this, but I am officially coining the 5Fs in school. Fight, flight, freeze, fawn, and flirt. The first four is scientifically backed, but my stupid brain thought that flirting is also a survival mechanism in school.
6. **The White Bear Effect (Wegner, 1994)**: When we try to suppress a thought, it becomes more intrusive. In turn, that's why trying to stop thinking of your crush makes you think of your crush.
7. **Physical Warmth and Emotional Warmth (Williams & Bargh, 2008)**: People who held a cup of hot coffee rated a stranger as having a warmer personality than those who held iced coffee.
   - The **dorsal posterior insula** (ignore the complex names if you don't understand) is the part of the brain that processes the temperature of your skin. The fronto-insular cortex processes trust, empathy, and social emotions. Since, they're neighbors, they can sometimes overlap and accidentally activate one another.
   - Lynott et al. (2008) tried to replicate the effect, but they found **zero** correlations between the two.
8. **The Mere Exposure Effect (Zajonc, 1968)**: You like things (and people) more simply because you see them often. It is because of familiarity!
9. **The Spotlight Effect (Gilovich et al., 2000)**: You think everyone notices your "flaws," but in reality, nobody does.
   - In a study, students had to wear an embarrassing T-shirt to a room. The students thought everyone noticed it, but in reality, fewer than 20% of people even saw it. Everyone else were busy thinking about their own embarrassing shirt.
   - Not an excuse to perform obnoxious actions in the public.
